About

A small, honest guide to the sky

Cosmos is a website for anyone who has ever looked up at night, seen a bright dot, and wondered what is that. It doesn't try to be the biggest astronomy site on the internet. It tries to be the friendliest one.

🪐

If you just want to learn

Every page is built to teach one thing well. The planets page lets you compare worlds side by side. The moon page tells you what phase the moon is in tonight, and why. The constellations page shows you what shape the stars are actually drawing when you look up.

💬

If you want to ask something

Beast is a chatbot that lives right here on the site. Ask it things like "how big is Jupiter compared to Earth?" or "what's that bright star next to the moon tonight?" It runs on a real AI, so you can talk to it like a person.

🧠

If you want to test what you know

The quiz section throws quick questions at you — planet sizes, lunar phases, the names of the constellations you've been staring at. It's short, and it doesn't keep score of you. It just wants you to learn something.

🌌

If you want to just look

Open the site, scroll slowly, and let the starfield do its job. There is no count of how long you've been here, no popups, no newsletter begging for your email. It's the web the way it used to feel.

Who built this

One student, one laptop, a lot of curiosity

Cosmos is made by a class-8 student who likes space and got tired of astronomy websites that either felt like textbooks or felt like they were written for grown-ups. This one is for everyone, but especially for the kid who just wants to know what they're looking at tonight.

Every page, every planet, every star you see here was put together by hand. The numbers come from NASA. The code was written one evening at a time. The chatbot is run on a real AI model, not a script pretending to be one. If something is wrong, it's wrong honestly — and there is a section below that says so.

Things worth knowing

Where the numbers come from

A site that teaches you about space should be honest about space. Here is what every number on this site is based on, and where the approximations are.

Planet facts

Diameters, distances, gravity, surface temperatures and orbital periods all come from NASA's planetary fact sheet — the same place professional textbooks pull from.

Moon counts move

Moon totals are not fixed. Saturn gained 128 new confirmed moons in 2025 and overtook Jupiter. If you see a moon count on this site and a year, the number is right for that year.

The moon phase is close, not exact

The phase calculation assumes the moon orbits in a perfectly even 29.5-day cycle. The real orbit speeds up and slows down a little, so the result can be a few hours off. Fine for looking up. Not fine for launching rockets.

Constellations are simplified

The star maps show the main stars and the shape most people recognize. Real constellations contain many more stars. The patterns are drawn to look right, not to catalogue precision.

Planets are sized right, space is not

The true-scale toggle on the planets page resizes every world correctly. The gaps between them are still nothing like real. Space is mostly emptiness, and emptiness does not fit on a screen.

Beast is honest about what it knows

Beast is an AI. Sometimes it is wrong. When you ask it a real question, it tries to give a real answer — but it will tell you when it is guessing, and it will point you back to the static pages on this site when it is not sure.

One more thing

Nothing here is tracking you

No analytics. No cookies. No accounts required to look around. The only account system on this site is the one you can sign into if you want to chat with Beast — and even then, nothing you say is stored or sold. The only links that leave this site are the ones in the footer, and they are clearly marked.

A small note on the build

This section is hidden at the bottom for the people who like to know how things were made. If that's not you, the rest of the site works perfectly well without it.

Cosmos is built with plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript. No frameworks, no build step, no install. The planets are drawn entirely in CSS — there is not a single image file in the whole project. The starfield is drawn live on a canvas. The constellations are generated from coordinates at runtime. The moon phase is calculated with arithmetic, not looked up. The whole folder can be opened by double-clicking index.html and it just runs.

If you want to change a colour, open css/style.css, find --teal at the top, and replace it with any colour you like. Save, refresh, and watch it ripple through every page.
